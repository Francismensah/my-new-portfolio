Writing Postmortems That Drive Real Change

Blameless is not the same as inconclusive. A good postmortem names what broke, assigns owned follow-ups, and makes the next incident cheaper — or impossible.

📅 2026 ⏱ 11 min read ✍️ Francis Morkeh Mensah
SRE Incident Postmortem Reliability

Why Most Postmortems Fail

I have read hundreds of incident documents titled “postmortem” that were really just polished timelines with a closing paragraph that said “we will do better.” Nobody owned anything. Nothing shipped. Six months later the same failure mode returned with a different error message.

Teams adopt “blameless culture” and accidentally produce accountability-less culture. The writeup becomes a performance of empathy instead of a contract for improvement.

A postmortem that drives change does three things well:

  • It explains the incident in terms a new engineer can understand.
  • It separates symptoms, proximate causes, and systemic contributing factors.
  • It ends with tracked action items — each with an owner, a priority, and a verification method.
💡 Rule of thumb: If your postmortem could be published without embarrassing anyone but also without changing any process, it is not finished.

When to Write It

Start within 48 hours of resolution while memories are fresh. Do not wait for perfect data — waiting is how incidents become folklore.

Timebox the meeting to 60–90 minutes. Longer sessions decay into therapy. Shorter ones skip the systemic layer.

  • SEV-1 / customer-visible: full postmortem required.
  • SEV-2 / major internal impact: full postmortem or lightweight review — pick one standard and stick to it.
  • Near-miss: short writeup still worth it; prevention is cheaper than outage.

The Structure That Works

I use the same skeleton for every serious incident. Consistency makes reviews faster and comparisons across incidents possible.

Section Purpose Common mistake
Summary What happened, who was affected, how long Burying the lede in technical detail
Impact Users, revenue, SLO burn, toil hours “Some users were affected” with no numbers
Timeline UTC timestamps, detection → mitigation → recovery Narrative prose with no times
Root cause The failure chain, not the first alert Stopping at “human error”
Contributing factors Process, design, capacity, observability gaps Treating as optional appendix
What went well Reinforce behaviors to keep Skipping entirely (morale matters)
Action items Owned, prioritized, ticket-linked “Improve monitoring” with no owner

Blameless, Not Vague

Blameless means we optimize for learning, not for protecting egos. It does not mean we avoid naming decisions.

Replace blame language with systems language:

Weak (blame-adjacent) Strong (systems-focused)
“Alice deployed the wrong config.” “Production config lacked schema validation; invalid values were accepted and propagated.”
“The on-call missed the alert.” “Alert routed to a low-signal channel; no paging policy matched SEV-1 latency burn.”
“Team B did not communicate.” “Incident commander role was undefined; status updates had no required cadence or template.”
If your root cause is only “operator mistake,” keep asking why the system allowed that mistake to reach production.

Root Cause vs. Contributing Factors

Root cause is the earliest point in the chain where a different decision or guardrail would have prevented harm — or reduced it materially.

Contributing factors are conditions that made the incident worse or slower to resolve: missing dashboards, unclear runbooks, on-call fatigue, dependency without timeout, change freeze ignored.

Example failure chain

Payment timeout incident (simplified)
Traffic spike
→
DB connection pool exhausted
→
Retries amplified load
←
Checkout errors · SLO burn

Symptom: checkout errors. Proximate cause: pool exhaustion. Systemic causes: no autoscaling signal on pool wait time, retry policy without jitter, load test never covered peak + retry scenario.

Action Items That Actually Ship

Every action item needs four fields. If one is missing, it will not ship.

  • Owner — one person, not a team mailing list.
  • Priority — P0 (prevent recurrence) vs P1 (reduce detection time) vs P2 (hygiene).
  • Ticket link — Jira, Linear, GitHub issue; visible in your incident tracker.
  • Done means — measurable: “alert fires within 2 min of pool > 80%,” not “improve docs.” 
action-item template# Example — good action item
Title:     Page when payment DB pool wait p95 > 500ms for 3m
Owner:     @platform-oncall
Priority:  P0
Ticket:    INC-4821 / PLAT-992
Done when: Alert tested in staging; runbook linked; fired in game day

# Example — weak action item (do not accept)
Title:     Be more careful with deploys
Owner:     Everyone
Priority:  —
Ticket:    —
Done when: —

Cap action items

More than five P0 items means nothing is P0. Prefer three high-leverage fixes over twelve aspirational ones. Defer hygiene to the backlog with honest priority.

Facilitation Tips

Whoever runs the meeting sets the tone.

  • Send the draft timeline and impact summary before the meeting.
  • Use a single notetaker; rotate the role so it is not always the incident commander.
  • Ask “what would have made this impossible?” not only “what broke?”
  • End with a read-aloud of action items and owners — verbal commitment matters.
  • Schedule a 30-day follow-up review in the same calendar series as the incident.
What good looks like: In the next quarterly review, you can point to closed tickets traced directly to postmortems — and incident recurrence rate for the same category drops.

Anti-Patterns to Reject

  • The novel — 40 pages nobody reads. Link deep dives; keep the core doc scannable.
  • The verdict — disguised performance review language in a “blameless” doc.
  • The infinite timeline — millisecond precision on irrelevant events; missing detection and customer impact times.
  • The ritual — postmortem filed because policy requires it, with no attendance from decision-makers.
  • The graveyard — 200 open action items, zero closed; team learns postmortems are theater.

Closing the Loop

Publish internally (and externally when appropriate). Tag the service, the failure category, and the quarter. Build a simple dashboard: open vs closed postmortem actions, age, repeat incidents by category.

Leadership signal matters. When an exec asks “what did we change after INC-4821?” and someone answers with a merged PR and a new alert, the culture sticks. When the answer is silence, the next postmortem will be thinner.

Key Lesson

A blameless postmortem is not a mood. It is a delivery mechanism for owned, verified improvements. Write less fluff, name the system, assign the work, and review the receipts in 30 days.
